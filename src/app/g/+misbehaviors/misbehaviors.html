Misbehaviors

a follow-up to the ballot didactics

by Gouvernathor, dec 2025

The spoiler effect you just learned about in the previous article is in fact well-known, although not always identified as such, by most politically active people, whether on the voter side or on the candidate side.

(Unless, of course, you live in a political system where there is no or almost no spoiler effect, such as the belgian or german proportional-based systems.)

This has consequences on the behavior of the political actors. Like game theory foresees, the rules make the players.

From encouraged infighting…

Under the First-Past-the-Post voting system, you have to choose a single candidate to vote for, you can't vote for several people. That logically implies that in order to get your vote, a candidate must convince you not no vote for any other candidate. Right?

This has the direct consequence that for a given candidate, it's much more rewarding to attack contenders that are close to their political values, because citizens who were poised to vote for them will be more easily convinced to switch their vote. (Whereas convincing people from across the political spectrum is high-effort, low-reward. Talking about the "other side" will more often be done in order to galvanize voters of your own political family, or as a "pick-me" strategy against your close adversaries.)

This effect fosters infighting within candidates that are politically close, from the same political family, which in return (assuming everyone maintains their candidacy) disadvantages that political family because of the spoiler effect, and can have the effect of dragging internal disputes to a public forum, which may draw undecided voters away from that political family altogether.

On the contrary, when you use grades-based systems, such as approval or score voting, liking a candidate is not synonymous with dragging another one down! And that's because these ballots allow the voter to give the same score (or approval) to several candidates, something even ranked voting doesn't allow.

Under these voting systems, there's no electoral gain obtained from dragging your adversaries down. Instead, they can be an incentive to run a campaign centered around a candidate's own merits, and to show what they bring to the table. Making for much more healthy discourse within your political family!

…to empowered hegemony

Within a given political family (the left, the right, the far-right...), leading to an election, candidates will be pressured not to split the vote. That was mentioned in the original article, with Bernie Sanders being pressured not to run as an independent in 2016 (which would have split the vote for the Democrats) - and also in 2020 for that matter.

There is a pressure for unity.

So, in general, for a given political family and a given election, there are three possible situations:

  • The candidates don't unite before the election, and the voters follow their honest preferences:
    • the vote is split and votes are spoilt, punishing the honest voters
  • The candidates don't unite and the voters behave strategically:
    • the vote is not split, but the voters are coerced to vote only for one of the two candidates deemed most likely to win (informally termed vote utile, the "useful vote", since votes for other candidates would be spoilt)
  • The candidates do unite before the election:
    • The voters don't get a full choice on the state-organized general election
    • Whether they're given a choice overall therefore depends on the democratic form of the unity process, more about that later

In practice, the first outcome will never happen (it will merge to some extent with the second one).

Also, note that since it depends on a candidate being designated the "useful vote", that effect is boosted when there's a single national majority election (e.g a presidential election). That's not so much the case in legislative elections, since the multiple electoral districts imply that you can't have the assurance to be the useful vote in all of them.

But let's take a specific practical situation: a party that feels itself likely to pass as the "useful vote" in the upcoming majority-voting election.

(Note: "majority-voting" means either First-Past-the-Post, or some fashion of two-rounds plurality/majority election. But it's a bit of an oversimplification : what I describe also applies to any electoral system with a spoiler effect.)

In that situation, it would be disadvantageous for that party to participate in a unity process (typically, a privately organized primary election) with other political parties… because losing that primary election is just an unnecessary risk for the party, strategically.

Alternatively, if the unitary process is a negociation with other organizations, then any concession made to those other organizations is a net loss that the party may just avoid, if they are confident enough of being regarded as the "useful vote" among their political family.

A political party in that situation is therefore pushed, only from the existence of a spoiler effect in the electoral system, to push towards the least democratic outcome of those I listed earlier: to coerce voters to vote for that party and no other.
Or, threatening to do just that, to force those other candidates to rally under them and turn these candidates into sycophants — which is not much better.

Both of these behavior are what's called chantage à l'union in french (which could be translated as "blackmail to unite" or "unity blackmail" — no, now's not the time to talk about Unity).

When one party adopts that strategy of bullying itself up the ballot, the other parties are coerced into supporting it, under the threat of being considered spoilers of the vote, and being sidelined and ostracized by the voters as a result.

If a challenger party (or group of political organizations) starts getting traction in a direction opposite the party embracing that strategy, it is also in the advantage of that would-be hegemonic party to smear the potential competitor, dragging it down to ensure there's no alternative, since again, the "useful vote" status is really an all-or-nothing — which boosts the infighting effect described earlier on.

The electoral system thus disfavors the most democratic outcomes (such as primary elections), and the political parties that practice democracy the most.
