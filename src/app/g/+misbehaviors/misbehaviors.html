Misbehaviors

a follow-up to the ballot didactics

by Gouvernathor, dec 2025

The previous article was instrumental in showing how the electoral system changes who is the winner of an election with a given electorate and candidates, and also how much the voters get to express their preferences. This is about how the electoral system impacts the relationships between the parties, how the political campaigns are run, and the inner working of the parties themselves.

This is about the consequences on the behavior of the political actors. Like game theory foretells, the rules make the players.

From encouraged infighting…

Under the First-Past-the-Post voting system, you have to choose a single candidate to vote for, you can't vote for several people. So, choosing a candidate also means selecting all but one candidate not to support. That logically implies that in order to get your vote, a candidate must convince you not no vote for any other candidate. Right?

This has the direct consequence that for a given candidate, it's much more rewarding to attack contenders that are close to their political values, because citizens who were poised to vote for them will be more easily convinced to switch their vote. Whereas convincing people from across the political spectrum is high-effort, low-reward. Talking about the "other side" will more often be done to galvanize your own supporters, or as a "pick-me" strategy against your close adversaries, rather than turning supporters of the "other side" to vote for yours.

This effect fosters infighting within candidates that are politically close, from the same political family, which in return (assuming everyone maintains their candidacy) disadvantages that political family because of the spoiler effect, and can have the effect of dragging internal disputes to a public forum, which may draw undecided voters away from that political family altogether.

On the contrary, when you use grades-based systems, such as approval or score voting, liking a candidate is not synonymous with dragging another one down! And that's because these ballots allow the voter to give the same score (or approval) to several candidates, something even ranked voting doesn't allow.

Under these voting systems, there's no electoral gain obtained from dragging your adversaries down. Instead, they can be an incentive to run a campaign centered around a candidate's own merits, and to show what they bring to the table. Making for much more healthy discourse within your political family!

…to empowered hegemony

Within a given political family (the left, the right, the far-right...), leading to an election, candidates will be pressured not to split the vote. That was mentioned in the original article, with Bernie Sanders being pressured not to run as an independent in 2016 (because it would have split the vote for the Democrats) — and also in 2020 for that matter.

There is a pressure for unity.

So, in general, for a given political family and a given election, there are three possible outcomes:

  • The candidates don't unite before the election, and the voters follow their honest preferences:
    • the vote is split and votes are spoilt, punishing the honest voters
  • The candidates don't unite and the voters behave strategically:
    • the vote is not split, but the voters are coerced to vote only for one of the two candidates deemed most likely to win across the whole election (informally termed vote utile, the "useful vote", since votes for other candidates would be spoilt)
  • The candidates do unite before the election:
    • The voters don't get a full choice on the state-organized general election
    • Whether they're given a choice overall therefore depends on the democratic form of the unity process — more about that later

In practice, the first outcome will never happen (it will merge to some extent with the second one).

Also, note that since it depends on a candidate being designated the "useful vote", that effect is less felt in the case in legislative elections, since the multiple electoral districts imply that a single party can't have the assurance to be the useful vote in all of them.
It is boosted when there's a single national majority election (e.g a presidential election).

Weaponizing the spoiler effect

But let's take a specific practical situation: a party that feels itself likely to pass as the "useful vote" in the upcoming majority-voting election.

(Note: "majority-voting" means either First-Past-the-Post, or some fashion of two-rounds plurality/majority election. But it's a bit of an oversimplification: what I describe also applies to any electoral system with a spoiler effect.)

In that situation, it would be disadvantageous for that party to participate in a unitary process (typically, a privately organized primary election in the case of a single single-winner election) with other political parties… because losing that primary election is just an unnecessary risk for the party, strategically.

Alternatively, if the unitary process is a negociation with other organizations, then any concession made to those other organizations is a net loss that the party may just avoid, if they are confident enough of being regarded as the "useful vote" among their political family.

A political party in that situation is therefore pushed, only from the existence of a spoiler effect in the electoral system, to aim towards the least democratic outcome of those I listed earlier: to coerce voters to vote for them and no other.
Or, threatening to do just that, to force those other candidates to rally under them and turn these alternative candidates into sycophants, which is not much better.

Both of these behavior are what's called chantage à l'union in french (which could be translated as "blackmail to unite" or "unity blackmail" — no, now's not the time to talk about Unity).

When one party adopts that strategy of bullying itself up the ballot, the other parties are coerced into supporting it, under the threat of being considered spoilers of the vote, responsible for giving the election to the other side, and being sidelined and ostracized by the voters as a result.

If a challenger party (or group of political organizations) starts getting traction in a direction opposite the party embracing that strategy, it is also in the advantage of that would-be hegemonic party to smear the potential competitor, dragging it down to ensure there's no alternative, since again, the "useful vote" status is really an all-or-nothing — which boosts the infighting effect described earlier on.

The electoral system thus disfavors the most democratic outcomes (such as primary elections), and the political parties that practice them the most.

Marginalizing the alternative

(the two-party system)

In reality, it will generally not be as clear-cut as either sidelining the adversary candidates by depriving them of voters, or forcing them to withdraw in favor of the bigger candidate, but rather a mix of the two (depending on the other candidates in question).

But the long-term consequence in systems with the stronger spoiler effects such as the single-round First-Past-the-Post election, is the shrinking number of (serious and viable) candidates, and of parties and political organizations in general — at least those involved in candidacies and elections.

As study cases, take the US, the Westminster system countries (Canada, United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand...), and Spain.

  • In the US, almost all legislative and presidential/gubernatorial elections are FPTP (with few exceptions across the states, and yes the presidential election uses a weird electoral college, but it's still an FPTP election).
  • In the Westminster system, all legislative seats are FPTP.
  • In Spain, while the Deputies are elected on a proportional basis, the electoral districts are so small (in number of seats) that it reintroduces a strong spoiler effect.

In all these countries, you will find two major parties dominating the political arena, switching power across legislatures for decades: Democrat/Republican, Liberal/Conservative, Labour/Conservative, Socialist-Worker's/People's…

And while in the Westminster countries or in Spain you can still have some other parties end up with sizeable shares of the legislative seats due to local differences between electoral districts (greens, lib-dems, scottish, québec or basque nationalists...), they generally have no hope of winning a plurality of the seats and form a government.

But worse, that's not even the case in countries that hold a single national (presidential) election, as in the US. Take France as an example: it uses a two-rounds majority election for national ballots, which has a theoretically lower spoiler effect than FPTP, but its presidential election makes its political landscape much more polarized than it otherwise would be — in fact, between the 1960's and the early 2010's, it could be described as having even more of a two-party system than many of the Westminster countries, or Spain.

All of that is due to the long-term bullying of the stronger parties, consolidating their power election after election and sidelining political diversity and opposition, being enabled by the electoral system and its spoiler effect.

How to fight this

Change the electoral system

No surprise there. Since the rules are the problem, change the rules.
But as a nuance to everything Nicky explained in the original article, I would advise shelving the uninominal ballot altogether whenever possible (meaning, an election for only one seat), as proportional representation is intuitive, it can be popular, and it erases most of the issues listed in the original article and even other problems not mentioned there.

Fight for democratic unity

Force your party, your political family, to use the most democratic alternative under the current system: common votes, common synthetic programs, primary elections…

Primary elections are usually how unity happens in majority systems. But there are other ways to foster democratic outcomes: sometimes, internal decisions or elections in a political party can decide how it will ally with others in future elections. That way, you can have terms for an alliance be negociated between party leaderships with a mandate to do so, without an actual primary election but still following a democratic process overall.

Don't let the bully always win, not without earning it, democratically.
